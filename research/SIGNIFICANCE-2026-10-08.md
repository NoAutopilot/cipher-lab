### Overview

Some, but not much. We count 68 items as read. A reviewer then tried to knock down every item the readers had rated above routine. After that review, none of the 68 is notable, 7 carry a useful detail a specialist could cite, and 61 are routine. The readers had rated three items notable, and the reviewer lowered all three, usually because the content was already in print or already legible.

Fifty-five of the 68 are Union telegrams from the 1864 Eckert telegraph books held at the Huntington Library, mostly quartermaster traffic about steamers, coal, horses and transports. For most of them, the plain-English words have been readable in the Huntington's public volunteer transcription since 2015-18. So our key mostly adds code words such as places, numbers, names and signatures, not the story itself. The other 13 are European diplomatic letters from 1530 to 1812. In those, the cipher hid the important part more often, but most of our readings are only partial.

### Closest to notable (all rated useful-detail)

1. **Chavigny to d'Avaux, Amiens, 25 Aug 1640 (Baluze 170 f.229).** *What it says:* France meant to reward Count Eberstein, the Hessian army's new commander after Melander gave up the post, to keep him loyal. It also wanted the Swedish envoy Salvius to work on a coded commander (probably Banér, the Swedish general) so as not to lose "the only adherents" France had in Germany. *Why it matters:* it is a hidden French instruction in the alliance politics of the Thirty Years' War. The command quarrel and Eberstein's appointment are already in print; what is ours is the coded intention. *Caveat:* every word of the Eberstein clause rests on signs graded uncertain. The key is Tomokiyo's. No prior decipherment located (verifier class N3).

2. **Fry to Manierre, 2 Nov 1864 (E37).** *What it says:* the Provost Marshal General told a serving provost marshal to withdraw as a candidate for Congress or resign, and sent the same text to his rival, the Union candidate Dodge. *Why it matters:* it is an order behind a withdrawal the press called voluntary, in a New York race later decided by a contested-election challenge. *Caveat:* the order, Fry's name and the copy to Dodge were already legible in the public transcription. Our key only fixed the district, the state and the titles, and the telegram gives no motive. N3.

3. **Quartermaster General's office to Allen, Louisville, 12 Dec 1864 (E146).** *What it says:* four days before the Battle of Nashville, Donaldson recommended that the military railroads take over the Louisville and Nashville Railroad. The Quartermaster General asked whether lending rolling stock would do instead. *Why it matters:* it is a proposal to seize a major private railroad at a crisis of the campaign. *Caveat:* the hesitation is in plain text, and the road's name, which our key supplies, could be guessed. The seizure never happened. The railroad's published histories are unread and may already discuss it. Weak N3.

### Useful details

- **August of Saxony to Orange, 18 Nov 1561 (WVO 57).** Two days after giving the Emperor's envoys a non-committal answer, the Elector told Philip II's stadholder in cipher that the Emperor wanted his son Maximilian elected King of the Romans, and asked him to keep it secret. This is our strongest European reading, made with a key rebuilt from a period decipherment (N4). The embassy itself is in print in Goetz 1891, and August's own Dresden draft is unseen and may hold the same text in plain.
- **Linhares dictionary code.** We identified the codebook behind a Portuguese book code of about 1811-12: an 1809 London pocket dictionary by Vieyra. That is a small, citable point for the history of cryptography. The note itself says little beyond "a secret even from the ministry" (N3).
- **Meigs to Ingalls, 24 July 1864 (N2-BI).** It gives a hard figure, 3,962 cavalry horses issued at Washington between 1 and 24 July. It also shows the Quartermaster General linking the government's cash shortage to slower horse deliveries.
- **Stanton to Peck, 3 Sept 1864 (E123).** The War Department offered to help private owners guard or arm their Long Island Sound steamers against a rebel plot to seize them. That offer is carried by the code words.

### Routine

The other 61 are logistics, staffing changes, movement notices, tips about suspects and fragmentary diplomatic reports. Their substance is either in print already or was legible without the key. This includes the 16th-century French letters (Gramont 1530, Danzay 1557/8, Birago 1572), which read only in fragments.

One fix is needed in the audit files: Colonel William Hamilton, the man named in E96, is already listed as a rebel agent in Horan's *Confederate Agent* (1954), so E96 should drop from N3 to N2.

### Where significance is more likely to come from next

The Huntington telegraph books will keep producing accurate but mostly routine readings, because their plain words are already public. The better odds are in letters where the cipher carried the political content and no period decipherment survives:

- more of the 1561-64 cipher enclosures between August of Saxony and Orange;
- further cipher passages in the d'Avaux and Chavigny letters in the Baluze volumes;
- Gramont's all-cipher letter to the King of 20 May 1530 (f.30), probably the separate "article" on Florence that the covering letter mentions, which so far reads only in fragments;
- Lodewijk van Nassau's letter 4612, not yet read, and the code 146 in WVO 5797.

Checks still owed:

- Lee (2011) for the Louisville and Nashville proposal.

## Lane SIG additions

- **Chavigny to d'Avaux, 25 Aug 1640 (Baluze 170 f.229), AUD-SIG-CHAV (account 3 verifier), 8 Oct 2026.** *What it says:* in cipher, the
  King will give Count Eberstein a gratification, now that he succeeds Melander at the head of the Landgravine's army, to bind him to do
  better than his predecessor; the dukes and the Landgravine want their troops joined to Longueville and kept under his command. *What it
  adds beyond print:* the reward itself is in print -- Louis XIII's letter to "le comte d'Heberstein", 9 Nov 1640, countersigned
  Bouthillier, sending La Boderie to give him "une marque du gré" (Caillet, *Correspondance historique et archéologique* 1912, pp. 65-66),
  and the succession is in Rommel VIII pp. 588-590 -- so the passage adds the decision and its stated purpose eleven weeks earlier, in
  the secretary of state's own despatch to the Hamburg envoy, not the fact of the reward. *Caveat:* the clause rests on letter signs
  graded M; key Tomokiyo's (published); class stays N3 (AAE volumes, the 1640 *Gazette* and Google Books not reached), depth D2.
- **Quartermaster General to Allen, Louisville, 12 Dec 1864 (eckert-1864 E146), AUD-SIG-E146 (account 3 verifier), 8 Oct 2026.** *What it
  says:* Donaldson recommended that the US Military Railroads take immediate possession of the Louisville and Nashville Railroad as vitally
  necessary to sustain the army; the Quartermaster General asked Allen whether he agreed, or whether lending USMRR rolling stock would do, and
  whether the company could not do all that was possible without the interruption of a change of hands, public freight and travel having
  preference. *What it adds beyond print:* the reports of all three officers named (Donaldson's of 30 June 1865, OR I/52 pt 1 p.680ff;
  Allen's of 1 July 1865, ibid. p.690; the QMG's in OR III/5) and McCallum's USMRR report (OR III/5) say nothing of it, and Herr's L&N
  history does not have it; the print shows only the aftermath (Allen to Meigs, 25 Dec 1864: Lyon's raid made the road useless for a week)
  and a 1865 transport report (p.715) counts the L&N among roads run by their own companies. So the telegram adds a take-over proposal
  and the Quartermaster General's apparent leaning toward leaving the company in charge, about two weeks before Lyon's raid cut the road. *Caveat:* Klein (1972) discusses the president's power to seize
  vital roads and was read only by snippet; Cotterill (AHR 1924) and Dalton (2018) unread; class stays N3 (weak), depth D2; key `period`.
