Ciphertext-only Attack on "Vatican Challenge" Ciphers (1625, 1628)

Challenge ciphertexts posted by Beáta Megyesi at MTC3 were brought to my attention by a German cryptology blog, Klausis Krypto Kolumne (7 March 2018). There were two ciphertexts for codebreaking:

Part 1: Letter of Giovanni Battista Pallotto (Wikipedia), papal nuncio, 1628

Part 2: Letter of a papal nuncio in France, 14 March 1625

A comment on the blog (8 March 2018) pointed out that the deciphered text was already available but, since I was working on similar Italian numerical ciphers then (see another article in Cryptologia), I tried ciphertext-only attack. It was successful for the Part 2 cipher but I had to rely on a crib for Part 1.

The MTC3 page announces that the solutions for these challenges were made public on 2018-06-20. (I have not seen them. I guess Ms Megyesi made a presentation at HistoCrypto 2018 at Uppsala, Sweden.) Since they are now public, I may also present my own solution here.

Solution of Vatican Challenge Part 1

Parsing

Both challenge ciphertexts consist of a sequence of continuously written figures, as was typical with Italian ciphers at the time (see another article). The first task is to split the stream of figures into symbols (groups of digits) corresponding to meaningful units in the cipher. For both cases, I succeeded in this before deciphering even a letter of the plaintext. The Appendix below gives transcription of the ciphertexts after this process.

For Part 1, statistics show the most frequent bigrams are 02, 20, and 60. When browsing the context of these, I noticed "20601962060", which seemed to be "20 60 196 20 60." That is, 196 is a three-digit symbol or 1+2 digit symbols. The special nature of "1" also seemed to be attested by the fact that, unlike the other digits, "1" never occurs in succession.

Although there remained some sequences of an odd number of digits, they were often surrounded by "1" or "8" and may be three-letter code symbols.

Deciphering

I spent many days trying to decipher the ciphertext, which now consisted of symbols of one- to three-digit figures. I came to a deadend. I had to give up my ciphertext-only attack and use the known plaintext. According to a blog post, the message begins with a sentence "Mandai come scrissi parte della mia famiglia con le robbe per Mantova."

Since I already had a ciphertext split into two- (or three-)digit figures, given this crib, there was no difficulty in assigning the plaintext letters as follows.

Where Did I Fail in My Statistical Analysis?

Contact Chart

From a contact chart, I thought 50,70,20,09,90,50,02,23,66 were vowels because they often occurred at the end of a word (i.e., before 1 and 8). (Here, I supposed 1 and 8 were nulls used as word separators.) Later, when marking these supposed vowels in the ciphertext, 00, 05, and 40 also seemed to be vowels because of alternation of vowels and consonants. Of these "vowels", relatively high frequencies of the sequence "50 70" suggested that 50=i and 70/20="a" or "o".

The correct values of these are as follows:

50(i) 70(o) 20(a) 09(e) 90(e) 50(i) 02(a) 23(s) 66(l), 00(a), 05(i), 40(i), in which 23 and 66 betrayed my prediction.

Solution of Vatican Challenge Part 2

Parsing

Again, the first task is to identify individual two-digit (and possibly one-digit and three-digit) symbols from the continuous stream of figures.

I identified 2 and 5 as single-digit symbols by pursuing a faint possibility, rather than through solid reasoning.

When I was examining instances of frequent tetragram "4033", I noticed "3654033" at the beginning of a line. Assuming that "40" and "33" were two-digit symbols and that a two-digit symbols is not broken at the end of a line, "365" should be either "3 65" or "36 5." When marking "5" in the ciphertext, the other digits seemed to consist of two-digit figures but too many exceptions were found.

I created a table of bigram frequency (a table in which, e.g., the cell at the intersection of row "4" and column "0" indicates the frequency of "40"; that is, here, a bigram refers to a pair of digits) and marked frequencies of 50 or higher. Then, I noticed both the row of "2" and the column of "2" had no marked cells. This seemed to indicate "2" was not used to form a two-digit symbol. (One may come up with many reasons to deny such a hypothesis but every faint possibility should be pursued in codebreaking.) The digit "5" seemed to behave similarly. Actually, the similarity was not so striking but somewhere in my mind, I thought "5" might be special.

So, I tried splitting the ciphertext with 2 and 5, which yielded many sequences consisting of an even number of digits and sequences of three digits. This seemed promising. The even number of digits consist of two-digit symbols and three-digit symbols are delimited by the single-digit symbols "2" and "5". That is, the single-digit symbols "2" and "5" seemed to be nulls.

More Systematic Approach for Parsing (by Thomas Bosbach)

Thomas Bosbach, who was the first to solve the Part 1 challenge and who also solved the Part 2 challenge before me, provided me with outlines of his method. His identification of single-digit symbols for the Part 2 challenge is more systematic than my approach above.

From the first, he assumed that the cipher contains mostly two-digit numbers as homophones representing the alphabet and two one-digit numbers as word separators. He "stumbled across" such a cipher in Meister, Die Geheimschrift im Dienste der Papstlichen Kurie (1906), p.71, as Argenti's cipher No. 5. His assumption was confirmed when he solved the Part 1 challenge in a (partly) known-plaintext attack.

He had no plaintext at hand for the Part 2 challenge but the assumption that two digits must be reserved as word separators allowed a systematic approach. If the ciphertext is broken with those two digits into segments, the segments must contain an even number of digits (possibly except for nomenclator entries, which may represent words or names in three digits). So Bosbach checked which combination of two digits as separators yielded the most groups of even length. Moreover, he could rule out some combinations through observation of the beginning or end of the lines. This way, he succeeded in identifying that "2" and "5" are single-digit symbols used as word separators.

Once these separators were identified, Bosbach could solve the ciphertext through trial and error, making use of the search and replace features of Microsoft Word.

Contact Chart

A contact chart showed some figures occurred frequently before "2" and "5", which seemed to indicate that "2" and "5" were word boundaries and those figures were vowels.

From this reasoning, I thought 40,18,16,44,06 were vowels.

Then, these supposed vowels were marked in the ciphertext. It led to further identification of 88, 77, and 36 as vowels for reasons of alternation of vowels and consonants. For example, "40 33 11 06 47 41 40 17 88 17 36" seemed to require 88 and 36 were vowels. (Of these, 88 and 77 were wrong. They turned out to be syllables.)

Pattern Matching

A sequence "2 19 41 36 19 41 39 44 522" has a promising repeating structure. The following match this pattern (I limited my search to those patterns that had 36 and 39 as vowels).

PROPRIA PROPRIE PROPRIO SCOSCIO TSUTSUI AGUAGLI CHECHIO ACIACHI

From this, I hypothesized 19(P) 41(R) 36(O) 19(P) 41(R) 39(I).

With these assignments,

"PRI80" indicated 80=MA

"19(P) 41(R) 18(?) 19(P) 40(?) 41(R) 04(?) 71 5" indicated "04=40=A"，"18=E".

Still, I was not sure. But then, "5 74 93(?) 41(R) 40(A) 41(R) 39(I) 36(O) 52" led to hypotheses 74=CON, 93=T, which was consistent with "5 74(CON)48ILIO 5", which in turn gave 48=C.

About this time, I felt sure I was on the right track.

After this, one identification led to others and most of the symbols were identified within a few hours.
