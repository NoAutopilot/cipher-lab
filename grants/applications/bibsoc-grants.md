# The Bibliographical Society (UK): Minor Grant now, Major Grant in the next cycle

## 1. The programme

- **Programme:** The Bibliographical Society, Grants and Fellowships for Research: Minor Grants (£50 to £300)
  and Major Grants (usually up to £2,000).
- **Pages (read 24 Sept 2026):** https://bibsoc.org.uk/fellowships/major-grants/,
  https://bibsoc.org.uk/fellowships/guidelines-applications/, https://bibsoc.org.uk/fellowships/application-procedure/
- **Deadlines:** Minor Grants "may be submitted at any time". Major Grants: the 2026 cycle closed on 16 Jan 2026;
  "We encourage interested applicants to apply when the next cycle opens in November 2026." Expect a deadline in
  mid-January 2027 (the 2026 one was 16 January, 11.59 pm GMT); check the page in November.
- **Amount:** Minor £50 to £300. Major "usually up to a maximum of £2,000 each"; about ten a year. "For
  applicants travelling from outside the UK and Europe, where no other funding is available, the Society may
  consider awarding additional funding (up to £1000) at its discretion."
- **Eligibility, quoted:** "Applicants for all awards may be of any nationality and need not be members of the
  Society or members of an academic institution or in full-time employment." And the subject: "scholars ... who
  are engaged in bibliographical research with primary focus on the physical object as historical evidence. This
  might include book history, textual transmission, the making and use of manuscripts ..."
- **What it will not fund (quoted):** "Applications in which literary, linguistic or broader historical topics
  dominate"; "Publishing costs, including those associated with digital imaging, reprographic permissions ..."
  It does fund "immediate research needs, such as for reproductions or travelling expenses".
- **Fit:** good, if the application is framed on the physical object. The project already works that way: its
  best results come from reading the manuscripts themselves (a decipherment written between the cipher lines in a
  second hand, a duplicate with the recipient's own decipherment, a key sheet filed in another box, a printed
  edition of 1742 that set the cipher in type). A pitch about "what the letters say" would fail the "broader
  historical topics dominate" test. The pitch below is about how cipher keys and decipherments were made, copied
  and used, as manuscripts.

## 3. Drafted copy

### 3a. Topic of research (field 7)

Cipher keys and decipherments as working manuscripts: how 16th- to 18th-century secretaries made, copied, filed
and used nomenclators, from the surviving key sheets, interlinear decipherments and duplicates

### 3b. Purpose (field 8)

Reproductions of manuscript leaves (key sheets, cipher letters with contemporary decipherments, and their
duplicates) from The National Archives, Kew, and German state archives, for a comparative study of how the keys
were made and used.

### 3c. Account of the research (field 13, no more than 1000 words; 700 words)

> Diplomatic letters of the 16th to 18th centuries often survive with part of their text in cipher. The ciphers
> were nomenclators: a table of numbers or symbols for letters, syllables, names and common words, written out by a
> secretary in two copies, one for each correspondent. What survives today is uneven. Sometimes the key sheet is
> filed with the letters. Sometimes the recipient's clerk wrote the decipherment between the cipher lines, or on a
> separate leaf, or on a duplicate sent by a second courier. Sometimes a later editor printed the cipher in type.
> Often nothing survives but the cipher itself.
>
> These traces are physical evidence of a working practice that is poorly described: who made the keys, how they
> were copied and corrected, how long one key stayed in use, how clerks deciphered (letter by letter, or by
> memory of common groups), and how deciphered text was filed and later printed. Answers to these questions sit in
> the manuscripts, in hands, inks, corrections and filing, rather than in the texts the ciphers conceal.
>
> Since September 2026 I have run an open research project, cipher-lab, that reads undeciphered cipher letters by
> recovering their keys from these physical traces. Its public repository (https://github.com/NoAutopilot/cipher-lab)
> holds a folder per manuscript with the page images used, a transcription, the recovered key, a script that
> regenerates the reading, and an audit of every printed and catalogue source searched. Examples show the range
> of evidence:
>
> - BnF Clairambault 1067 fol. 226r-227r (Loménie de Brienne to the Queen of Poland, 1646): a contemporary
>   interlinear decipherment over every cipher line gave an 84-code key.
> - BnF fr. 5160 fol. 86-88 (Brienne to Servien, 1659): one leaf deciphers the passages of another; the key
>   recovered from it reads a third letter in the same volume.
> - Huntington Library mssDE 108(A) and 108(B) (La Luzerne to Destouches, 1781): a duplicate carries the
>   recipient's own decipherment.
> - Huntington Library mssBLA 176-195 (Madrid intelligence, 1725-29): seven items with contemporary decipherments
>   yield a key of 395 code groups that reads three items without them.
> - Birch's edition of the Thurloe State Papers (1742) printed cipher letters with their decipherments above the
>   cipher groups, and in one case the cipher alone.
>
> The larger project is a comparative record of these traces. For each manuscript I record where the key
> evidence sits (on the leaf, in the same volume, elsewhere), in which hand, whether the key was used
> consistently or corrected, and how the text reached print, if it did. Comparing across archives and decades
> shows how nomenclator practice worked in the French, English, Dutch and Imperial chanceries. The results will be
> written up as an article for *The Library* or a comparable journal, and all data will stay public in the
> repository.
>
> **What the grant would pay for.** The next group of manuscripts are held where no free images exist, and I can
> see them only through the archives' reproduction services:
>
> - The National Archives, Kew, SP 99/24/251 (State Papers Venice: a cipher letter of 1622 catalogued as from
>   "159", in Sir Henry Wotton's hand, with a sibling leaf, f.159, to be examined for a key or decipherment) and SP 81/37/284 (a 1631 "duplicate of a
>   paper sent by Mr Stanning, in cipher": a duplicate is the kind of copy on which a second decipherment can
>   survive).
> - Generallandesarchiv Karlsruhe: two cipher letters of 1633 from Archduchess Claudia de' Medici to Margrave
>   Wilhelm of Baden-Baden.
>
> For each I will record the physical evidence of key-making and use, and test whether a key from a sibling item
> reads it.
>
> **Estimated breakdown of costs (£300).**
>
> | Item | Estimate |
> |---|---|
> | TNA, SP 99/24/251 and f.159: digital copies (page check first, £9.92 per the TNA order form used for SP 105/65 on 19 Sept 2026) | £60 |

>
> No travel or subsistence is requested. The copies are for research only; nothing is requested for publication

- Form: not yet published for the 2027 cycle. The 2026 procedure asked for the Society's form plus "Two referees
  familiar with the applicant's work", who email references to the Secretary by the deadline.
- Guidelines to meet: "a clear and detailed breakdown of how and where the money will be spent (e.g. airfares must
  be separated from accommodation; reproduction costs must be listed separately, etc.). The number of days for
  which accommodation is required must be stated clearly. The maximum allowed for daily accommodation is usually
  £100.00 ... subsistence costs (meals etc.) are not supported."

  evidence (TNA, BL, German and Swedish archives), plus, if you can travel, five days at Kew or the British Library
  to examine key sheets that are not worth ordering blind. Reuse 3c, extended with the minor grant's findings.
- Two referees: ask in November for a January deadline.

Minor Grant: fill the Society's Word form (download it from the application-procedure page) and email it to
bursaries@bibsoc.org.uk from your own address; your referee emails separately. No portal. A local agent can fill
the Word form on your machine with you watching; you send the email. A cloud session must not send it.
