## 3. Drafted copy

### 3a. Title

Reading the Union's cipher telegrams: the Eckert ledgers and cipher books, 1862-1867, and the Huntington's
earlier diplomatic ciphers

### 3b. Project description and significance (about 1,000 words; 959 words)

> During the Civil War the United States Military Telegraph sent the War Department's most sensitive messages in
> cipher. The system replaced names, places, dates and common phrases with code words ("arbitraries") taken from a
> cipher book, and, in its full form, scrambled the word order along a route fixed by a keyword. Thomas T. Eckert,
> who ran the War Department telegraph office, kept the office's ledgers of telegrams as sent, with their code
> words, and the cipher books themselves. They came to the Huntington as the Thomas T. Eckert Papers (mssEC 1-76).
>
> Between 2016 and 2017 the Huntington's Zooniverse project "Decoding the Civil War" transcribed the ledgers with
> volunteers. Its planned second phase, decoding, never launched. The transcriptions and images are public, and
> many entries can be half read as they stand, but the code words that carry the names, places and key phrases
> were never resolved. A historian who wants to know exactly what Washington told a commander on a given night
> still has to find the text in the Official Records, if it was printed there, or resolve the code words by hand.
>
> I am an independent researcher who runs a small open project, cipher-lab, on historical ciphers. Since September
> 2026 I have read cipher letters from European and American archives by recovering the keys that survive beside
> them and applying them with scripts that anyone can re-run. My working method has three rules. First, every
> decoded word carries a grade that says where it came from: read from a key, read from a known plain text, or
> inferred. Second, a result is not called new until a separate search of printed editions, catalogues and
> scholarship has failed to find it, and that search is logged. Third, no negative result is reported unless the
> same method succeeds on a test cipher built to match.
>
> The Eckert papers were my project's pilot. Working from the public images and transcriptions, I rebuilt the
> keys of Cipher No. 1 and No. 2 from the filled-in cipher book mssEC 41, and read twenty entries of the 1864
> "Ciphers Sent" ledger (mssEC 19). Every reading was checked against print. Most proved to be telegrams already
> printed in the Official Records or in Lincoln's Collected Works, which is itself useful: it links ledger entries
> to their printed texts and tests the key. Two telegrams to General Butler of 21 and 22 April 1864, from Assistant
> Secretary of the Navy Fox and from Quartermaster General Meigs, have not turned up in print as sent, after
> searches of the Official Records, the Official Records of the Navies, Butler's published correspondence and
> Lincoln's Collected Works; their substance is printed, their text as enciphered is not.
>
> The fellowship would let me extend this from twenty entries to the ledgers as a whole, and in doing so answer
> questions that matter to historians of the war and of communications.
>
> First, how much of what the War Department sent in cipher reached print? The Official Records were compiled
> from received copies and office files. The ledgers are the sending office's own record. Aligning the two, entry
> by entry, shows what the printed record left out, and where it altered wording or dates.
>
> Second, how did the ciphers change, and how did clerks use them? The ciphers were reissued with new code words
> and routes over the war. The Huntington holds the books in which the clerks worked. Which cipher was in use
> on which date, and how consistently the operators followed it, can only be settled from the books themselves:
> marginal corrections, pasted slips, and the order in which code words were added.
>
> Third, what did the office send that the printed record does not contain at all? Only a full pass will tell,
> and only a full pass can give a figure. My pilot suggests most traffic is printed and a minority is not; the
> exact share is the result.
>
> A second, smaller strand uses the Huntington's older diplomatic ciphers. The Blathwayt papers addenda (mssBLA)
> hold intelligence from Madrid and Port Ste Marie of 1725-1729, several items with contemporary decipherments.
> From those decipherments I recovered a key of 395 code groups and read the ciphered lines of three further items
> for which no prior decipherment was located. The Destouches papers (mssDE) hold the French minister La Luzerne's
> cipher letters to the naval commander Destouches in 1781, one of them with Destouches's own decipherment beside a
> duplicate. Both collections show how 18th-century diplomats used numbered code books, and both reward a reader
> who can compare the physical items side by side.
>
> The method is quick once the key is certain, and slow where it is not. What slows it is exactly what a remote
> reader cannot see: faint pencil in the cipher books, items not imaged or imaged only in part, and loose sheets
> whose order matters. Two months in the reading room would let me check every key against the original books,
> resolve the doubtful readings, and record the physical evidence (hands, inks, insertions) that a scan loses.
>
> The results will be public. Each reading has a script that regenerates it from the transcription and the key,
> and an audit file that lists every source searched. The Huntington would receive the keys and the ledger-to-print
> concordance for its catalogue and for any future phase of the Zooniverse project, and historians would have a
> searchable plain-text index to the War Department's sent cipher traffic.
>
> This is a project for a nonspecialist panel in one respect above all: its product is a text historians can read.
> Nobody needs to know how a Civil War cipher works to use a concordance that says "this telegram, sent at this hour,
> said this, and here is where it was or was not printed."

### 3c. Specific Huntington materials to be consulted (no more than 350 words; 197 words)

> **Thomas T. Eckert Papers (mssEC 1-76).** The "Ciphers Sent" and received ledgers, 1862-1867, starting with
> mssEC 15 (1 Feb-21 July 1862) and mssEC 19 (1864), which my pilot used; the filled-in cipher books, starting with
> mssEC 41 (Cipher No. 1) and the books for the later ciphers; and the ledger mssEC 25, which holds a second copy of
> the two April 1864 Butler telegrams. [Before submitting: list the other cipher-book numbers from the finding aid,
> and mark which of them are not imaged online.]
>
> **Blathwayt Papers addenda (mssBLA 176-195).** The Madrid and Port Ste Marie intelligence of 1725-1729:
> BLA 179, 185, 187, 188, 189, 190 and 194 (items with contemporary decipherments) and BLA 184, 186 and 191(a)
> (cipher items read from the key recovered from them), to check the key against the originals and look for the
> key sheet itself.
>
> **Charles-René-Dominique Sochet Destouches Papers (mssDE 1-120).** La Luzerne's cipher letters of 16 and 31
> January 1781 (mssDE 68, mssDE 108(A)) and the duplicate mssDE 108(B) with Destouches's decipherment, and any key
> or code book in the collection.
>
> **Reference.** The Huntington's copies of the Official Records and related printed series, used to check
> readings against print on site.

### 3d. Plan of work (no more than 350 words; 188 words)

> **Before arrival.** Build the full ledger index from the Zooniverse transcriptions and my existing keys; mark
> every entry by status (read and printed, read and not found in print, not yet read, doubtful). This is scripted and
> done at home.
>
> **Weeks 1-2.** Cipher books. Check every code word and route of Cipher No. 1 and No. 2 against mssEC 41 and the
> later books; record reissues and dates of use; photograph (where permitted) the pages that the online images
> miss.
>
> **Weeks 3-5.** Ledgers. Work through the doubtful and not yet read entries at the originals, in date order; settle each
> against the right cipher; record hands, insertions and corrections.
>
> **Week 6.** Blathwayt and Destouches. Check the 395-group key against the originals of BLA 179-194; look for the
> key sheet; compare mssDE 108(A) and (B).
>
> **Weeks 7-8.** Concordance and print check. Align every read telegram with the Official Records and Lincoln's
> Collected Works; run the logged search for each telegram not found; write up the findings for the Huntington's
> curators.
>
> Each week's readings are pushed to the public repository with their audit logs, so the curators can follow the
> work as it goes.

### 3e. Project output

> 1. A public concordance of the Eckert sent-cipher ledgers: each entry with its decipherment, grade, cipher used
> and printed source, or the logged search that failed to find one.
> 2. Machine-readable keys for the Eckert ciphers and the Blathwayt code, offered to the Huntington's catalogue and
> to the Zooniverse project's team.
> 3. An article for a journal of Civil War or intelligence history (for example *Cryptologia* or *Civil War
> History*) on what the sending office's record adds to the printed record.
> 4. A talk or blog post for the Huntington, if the curators wish.
