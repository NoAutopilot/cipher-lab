A Syllabic Cipher (ca.1715) of Cardinal Gualterio Reconstructed Manually

Cardinal Gualterio was one of the closest advisors to James Francis Edward Stuart, known as the Old Pretender, who sought to regain the British throne which his father James II forfeited. Many of his letters are preserved in the British Library. Though his correspondence in cipher with Saint-Simon is now lost (Wikipedia), the DECODE database has some papers in cipher from his correspondence with others.

Letters of Abbé Jean D'Estrées

Add MS 20359 (DECODE R8219-R8252), Add MS 20360 (R8253-R8294), Add MS 20361 (R8295-R8339) include letters of the abbé J.D. Estrées or Jean D'Estrées (Wikipedia) (vol. 1: January 1703-May 1716, vol. 2: June 1716-March 1717, vol. 3: April 1717-February 1718). D'Estrées (1666-1718) was a member of the Council of Foregin Affairs, set up upon the death of Louis XIV under the Regent, Duke of Orleans.

The first four letters (R8219-R8222) are in an incredibly simple cipher (monoalphabetic substitution plus a few code symbols), while the remaining letters use a fuller code. To look more closely, the code used in later letters employ the same substitution alphabet as the first four but additionally employ many symbols for syllables.

It is not clear whether the code was updated some time between the first four (R8219 and R8220 are from Madrid, 1707) and the letters from 1715-1718, or the enciphering clerk for the first four letters simply did not make full use of the syllabic cipher. To support the latter position, the last letter (R8339 from 28 February 1718, three days before his death) is again almost monoalphabetic. The old style of "5" used in this last letter and the first four letters seems to indicate these were enciphered by someone different from those who enciphered the other letters. Moreover, the Medinaceli letters also used the almost monosyllabic cipher in 1706-1710 (see the next section).

Manual Reconstruction of Syllable Cipher

The monoalphabetic substitution table can be readily reconstructed from the interlinear decipherment in R8219. The syllable symbols could have been difficult, but because of the regularity in the assignment, once a few symbols were revealed, there was no difficulty in reconstructing the whole table. The code symbols for the nomenclature are yet to be identified for the most part.

While new computer algorithms can brreak syllable codes (see my blog), the manual reconstruction of syllable codes may be of some interest. (My work was based on R8223 and R8224.)

When the substitution alphabet 1-21 was identified, a lot more symbols remained unknown, but I was sure most of 22-170 were syllables. I also knew that syllable symbols can be CV and VC.

Symbols for syllables can be recovered if one can spot a word where only one syllable remains unidentified.

19(s) 1(o) 18(u) 6(h) 10(a) 8(i) 94(?) revealed 94=te. Then, it was straightforward to identify 136=om from 3(c) 136(?) 5(p) 94(te) 12(r).

Then, 8(i) 10(a) 22(?) 84(re) 15(n) 52(?) 46(?) 2(m) 5(p) 94(te) 10(a) reveals 46=co and then 22=y and 52=du. (I had not been aware that 22 was in the substitution alphabet because it did not occur in the interlinear decipherment of the short specimen in R8219.)

17(q) 99(?) 8(i) 21(e) revealed 99=ue, confirmed by 8(i) 21(e) 99(ue) 18(u) 20(x).

14(t) 109(?) 5(p) 19(s) 167(?) 5(p) 84(?) suggested 109=em and 84=re. 167 may represent some word but later turned out to be a null. (Some of my initial hypotheses were not solid and had to be corrected later. At first, 21(e) 20(x) 5(p) 85(?) led me to think 85=re, but this turned out to be 85=ri.) 5(p) 84(re) 89(?) 15(n) 14(t) revealed 89=se. 18(u) 140(?) 14(t) 84(re) indicates 140=os. 113=er was also identified (maybe from 84(re) 19(s) 113(?) 18(u) 21(e)).

Content of D'Estrées' Letters

D'Estrées generally sent his reports on a weekly basis. The recipient is called "V[otre] E[minence]", which probably refers to Cardinal Gualterio (Wikipedia). The metadata in the DECODE database says "mostly ciphertext with cleartext and letters in plaintext", which seems to indicate the plaintext is filed in the volume, but the plaintext letters are not in the DECODE database.

D'Estrées' letters record real-time reactions to historical events such as James Edward's expedition to Scotland (December 1715), Prince Eugene's victory over the Turks (August 1716), Spanish invasion of Sardinia (August 1717), and the Triple Alliance (January 1717). (Actually, these are mentioned in cleartext. Hopefully, the ciphertext contains even more interesting contents.)

The following presents excerpts of a line or two from each letter, to show they are indeed in the same cipher. Two, used for reconstructing the cipher, are deciphered in full.

Gualterio-Torcy Cipher (1718)

Gualterio's letter in Italian to the French Foreign Minister Torcy, dated Rome, 19 April 1718, in cipher with interlinear decipherment, allows reconstruction of the code. The following is based on the analysis (including alignment of ciphertext and decipherment as well as the outline of the content) kindly provided by Alexandre Pillon.

The letter concerns Rakoczi (Wikipedia). Supported by the French, he led the Hugarian rebels against the Hapsburgs during the War of the Spanish succession. After the change of regime in France upon the death of Louis XIV, he went to the Ottoman Empire in 1717, but the latter signed the Peace of Passarowitz with Austria in July 1718.

The letter informs Torcy of a move, purportedly instructed by Torcy himself, to negotiate with Venice to help Rakoczi and asks for instructions.
