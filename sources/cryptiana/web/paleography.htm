Paleography in Examples: Tips for Reading Old Handwriting

Old handwriting is hard to read for modern readers. Introduction to the art is found in websites such as the one by the British National Archives, Spanish Paleopgraphy, etc. (The BNA website presents ten documents as tutorials, of which document 1 is in "italic" style, which formed the basis of the modern writing, document 2 is in cursive style, documents 3, 4, 5, 7, 9 are in secretary hand, document 8 is a mixture of late secretary hand and italic hand, document 6 is in a legal hand (used in the Exchequer), and document 10 is in Chancery hand.)

The present article presents specimens of reading old handwriting from the French and Spanish archives.

First Things You Should Know When Reading Old Handwriting

Here are the first things you should know when reading any old documents.

  • Letters "u" and "v" are often interchangeable. So are "i" and "j" (and even "y"). Even in the late 18th century, dictionaries listed words in an order such as "idyl" - "jealous" - "if" - "ignore" - "jig" ... or "vaunt" - "ubiquity" - "veal" ....
  • Letter "s" is written like an "f" without the horizontal stroke (ſ). This is called "long s" and occurs at the beginning or the middle of a word. As it turns out, the long s may be one of the most recognizable letters in the document below.
  • Spelling was not standardized. In the example below, the spelling in the decipherment is sometimes different from the spelling of the ciphertext.
  • Abbreviations may be used.
  • Nasal sound ("n", "m") may be represented by a curve over a vowel.
  • Descenders (as in "p", "y", "g") and ascenders (as in "d", "h") help identifying the letter. Beware that the convention was different in old handwriting (see below).
  • Letters without descenders nor ascenders (including those called "minims") are the hard ones.

Some Tips for Old French Handwriting

  • Vowel "o" is used in the inflectional ending where "a" is used today. So "avait" was written "avoit", which is more consistent with the infinitive "avoir." (I wonder how it was pronounced.) Examples abound, e.g., line (2) in the French letter (1593) below . This may not be related to the infinitive because "etaient" was written "etoient."
  • There seem to be special symbols for "que" and "et". See line (3) etc.
  • Letter "s" often occurs where a circumflex (^) is in modern French. So "même" was written "mesme" as in line (1). See "plustost" on line (8) and "estre" on line (9).
  • There are other intercalary letters such as "c" in "faict" (see line (10) etc.) and "scavoir"; "l" in "aultre"; "s" in "escrit" (see line (16)). At least some of these are reminiscent of Latin spelling. See also "congnoistre" on line (8) and "congneu" on line (11). The latter reads "cogneu" in the ciphertext.

(More details may be found at Cours de paléographie.)

Specific Notes on the Specimen Below

(Some of the following may be general enough to deserve promotion to the above sections.)

  • Some letters ("n", "h", "x") have descenders not found in modern writing. This is somewhat similar to the modern script style of "z", which has a descender not found in "z" in print. See lines (1)(4)(6) etc. for "n", line (6) for "h" and "x".
  • Line (1), note the abbreviation "vre" (votre).
  • "v" as in "vre" has a flourish to the upper left.
  • Note the almost undistinguishable forms of "e", "c", "r", etc. In "escrypt" on line (1), "r" looks like a round "v".
    Letter "e" at the end of a word looks like "c" in "Altesse" and "mesme" or "v" in "ceste".
    Letter "t" looks like "c" in "escrypt" and "present" and like a mere vertical stroke in "Altesse". On line (4), the two "t"s differ in form in "tout."
  • Line (2), the word "lettre" is abbreviated ("lre"). A curve over a word indicates abbreviation in "pre[san]té".
  • Line (2), the word "pour" is abbreviated as "p" plus a curve.
  • Line (3), "q" looks like "g". Compare it with a real "g" on line (4).
  • Line (4), the leftward stroke of "i" and the loop of "l" makes "il" look like "je"
  • While "de" on line (2) is legible, "de" near the end of line (4) looks like "le" because of the loop of the ascender of "d".
  • Line (7), the ending "-eur" is abbreviated.
  • Line (9), the ending "-aire" in "necessaire" is abbreviated. Similarly for "faire" on line (8) and "affaire" on line (10).
  • Line (10), The first "p" in "apparance" look like a "y".

Roman Numerals

Roman numerals (which are frequenty used for dates in old letters) can be tricky. In the following example, "ii" looks like "y"; "iii" looks like "uy" (minus a stroke); "iiii" looks like "uy" or "my".

In the example below as well as others in the same cipher collection, the roman numerals for "80" and above reflect the numerals of the French language: "80" is not written as lxxx but as iiiixx (that is, "quatre-vingt") and "90" as iiiixxx (that is, "quatre-vingt dix"). While "c" for "100" and "cx" for "110" are the same as in the modern convention, "120" is not written as "cxx" but as "vixx" (6*20), "130" is "vixxx", and "140" is "viixx" (7*20).


In the following, "ii" looks like "n"; "iii" looks like "m"; and "iiii" looks like "nn." Moreover, "x" looks like a cursive "e". (Such a style in writing "x" is also seen in the letter in BnF fr.3984, f.7 above. See line (18).)


Roman numerals used in Spanish ciphers around 1500 (see another article) were used with a convention not mentioned in Wikipedia. Specifically, Roman numerals with a maximum number of "i" are marked with o, presumably for easy recognition: e.g., iiiio; viiio (viiii is not used)). CC and CCC are in ligature. M is written like "IU" and MM is written like "IIU." (I do not know whether these conventions were widely used.)

My Progress

(March 2021) The other day, I found among my records my pour transcription made many years ago. Now, I can more or less read the handwriting without much difficulty. Although it's still hard for me to read old handwriting, I'm gratified of my progress in the last decade or so.

(NEW) Vous verrez monseigneur par ce que le conte de carpy escript au Roy des propos quil eut auant hier avec ...
