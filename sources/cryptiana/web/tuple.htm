Tuple Cipher -- Enciphering a Letter with Multiple Letters

When a ciphertext is a stream of symbols, it is a usual assumption that the symbols are units of encryption.

In case of Arabic figures, it is quite common that a figure consisting of multiple digits forms a unit. Such units should be visible by space in-between, or consist of a constant number of digits and be thus readily separable. An important exception is a series of numerical ciphers used in Italy, where symbols consisting of a variable number of digits are written continuously without break. Such variable-length numerical ciphers are reported in S.Tomokiyo (2017-2019), "Variable-Length Symbols in Italian Numerical Ciphers" (another article) and Lasry et al. (2020), "Deciphering Papal Ciphers from the 16th to the 18th Century" (Cryptologia).

It is also common that alphabetical letters form multi-letter code groups, as in Spanish ciphers (see another article), but it's not so common to use multi-letter groups in a substitution alphabet.

Matignon's Tuple Cipher (1586)

Some years ago, I reconstructed a French cipher from 1586 in which each letter of the alphabet is represented with a tuple (i.e., set) of two to four symbols/letters/digits, which I called a tuple cipher (see another article)


Ascanio Sforza Visconti's Tuple Cipher (ca. 1480s)

As to letters, Cardinal Ascanio Maria Sforza Visconti's Tuple Cipher (ca. 1480s) represents a letter in the alphabet with a combination of two letters. (Two ciphers I found may actually be the same cipher or two variants of the same cipher, at least in the substitution alphabet.)


(About the dating, both R5313 ("Carazolus et Salicetus Anoniros[?] Card. [A]scanio M[ari]a Sforza Visconti") and R1859 (I8542) "Carle Sforza Vesconte" indicate 1484-1505, when Ascanio Sforza Visconti was cardinal. The latter's nomenclature has an entry "Duchessa di Callabria", probably Ippolita Maria Sforza, who died in 1488. The dating to "ca.1480s" is also consistent with "Carazolus et Salicetus" (Carazolus et Bartholomaeus Salicetus), whose letter from 1498 ("lettera degli ambasciatori Caracciolo e Saliceto a Ludovico Sforza") is in Archivio della R. Società Romana di Storia Patria (1894), vii, p.333 (Internet Archive).)


Two more of such tuple ciphers are reported in Lasry, Simonetta, and Biermann (2025), "Antonio Elio 'Cipher' and his Polyphonic-Syllabic Cipher" (HistoCrypt 2025)

Giovanni Guidiccioni's Tuple Cipher (1535-1537)

One is a cipher used in the correspondence of Giovanni Guidiccioni, papal nuncio in Spain, with Pope Paul III or the Farnese Curia (1533-1537). Its symbols for alphabetical letters consist of one or two letters, while the syllables are also represented by one or two letters.


Interestingly, syllables "da", "de", "di", "do", "du" are all represented by "d"; "pa", "pe", "pi", "po", "pu" are represented by "p"; and "ta", "te", "ti", "to", "tu" are represented by "t." The omission of the vowels in these symbols is as if the cipher is polyphonic in a limited way. But this is different from typical polyphonic ciphers in which two or more letters in the alphabet are represented by the same cipher symbol. (See another article for typical examples and another article for various instances).

Filiberto Ferrerio's Cipher (1537-1540)

Another is a cipher used in letters of Filiberto Ferrerio, Bishop of Ivrea and papal nuncio in France (1537-1540).

In this cipher, a letter in the alphabet is represented by a combination of two letters/symbols and syllables are represented by single letters/symbols. Such symbols, written continuously without break, made it difficult to identify the cipher, even though interlinear decipherment is provided.


Moreover, this cipher uses the same symbol for two syllables, of which one is distinguished from the other by a dot placed over the preceding symbol. To complicate matters, such dots are often omitted. The omission seems too often to be enciphering errors, as if the syllabic representation were polyphonic.

(According to Lasry et al. (2025), it is "highly likely" that this interesting syllabic cipher was devised by Antonio Elio, a cipher secretary of Cardinal Farnese. He is credited with the invention of polyphonic ciphers (the earliest known published polyphonic cipher was the one used by Cardinal Farnese, printed in Meister (1906), p.222) and his other ciphers are in J. Lestocquoy (1966), Correspondance des nonces en France. Dandino, Della Torre et Trivultioo (1546-1551), p.42-45. During his long service in the curia (1532-1572), he trained Giovanni Battista Argenti. He was also known for his codebreaking talent.)
