# Leads on the Untersberg prophecy inscription

These are leads, not a verdict. The evidence points to a transmission problem with at least three separable objects: the six-line HS 2398 inscription, shorter strings of initials in other witnesses, and later expansions attached to those shorter strings. Conflating them is the main error risk.

## 1. Period-appropriate Latin/German abbreviation references

**Best reference pair.** Use Cappelli as a sign-form index, but pair it with Walther because Cappelli's own German preface identifies Johann Ludolf Walther as the predecessor grounded in German archives. Cappelli also warns that general abbreviation rules have exceptions by time and place. His instructions explain how to look up a form by its retained letters and then verify date, sign, and expansion rather than searching only a guessed word.[1] Walther covers diplomatic abbreviations in codices and documents from the eighth through sixteenth centuries and is therefore the most directly relevant historical German comparator located in this search, although the pertinent plate/column for **symA** is unverified.[2]

- **Adriano Cappelli, _Lexicon abbreviaturarum: Dizionario di abbreviature latine ed italiane_, 2nd German ed. (Leipzig, 1928), pp. V–VIII and 1–6.** Full scan: https://archive.org/details/LexiconAbbreviaturarum . The Archivio di Stato di Torino also describes the work and its limits: https://archiviodistatotorino.cultura.gov.it/strumenti/dizionario-abbreviature-italiane/ .[1]
- **Johann Ludolf Walther, _Lexicon diplomaticum: abbreviationes syllabarum et vocum in diplomatibus et codicibus a seculo VIII ad XVI usque occurrentes exponens_ (Ulm, 1756), relevant plate/column unverified.** Full-view record: https://books.google.com/books?id=ZItMzqEqxPAC .[2]
- **Paul Arnold Grun, _Schlüssel zu alten und neuen Abkürzungen: Wörterbuch lateinischer und deutscher Abkürzungen des späten Mittelalters und der Neuzeit_ (1966), relevant page unverified.** Bibliographic lead only; edition and scan access should be checked before use: https://search.worldcat.org/search?q=ti%3ASchl%C3%BCssel+zu+alten+und+neuen+Abk%C3%BCrzungen+au%3AGrun .[3]

**Adversarial limitation.** HS 2398 is dated 1690–1710, later than Walther's stated range and later than many Cappelli exemplars. Neither dictionary can by itself establish the sign's value. A match should be accepted only if the same hand uses the sign elsewhere with a recoverable expansion.

## 2. Modern edition, translation, or scholarly discussion

Several items go substantially beyond Herzog's 1929 collation:

1. **Yvonne Weber-Fleischer's transmission study** is the strongest lead: “Die Überlieferung von den Herrschern im Berg – dargestellt am Beispiel der Untersbergsage,” in Ulrike Kammerhofer-Aggermann (ed.), _Sagenhafter Untersberg. Die Untersbergsage in Entwicklung und Rezeption_ (Salzburg, 1992), pp. 17–170.[4] The subject and page span suggest a manuscript census and stemmatic discussion; check its notes and appendices for an edition of HS 2398 and for sigla absent from Herzog.
2. **Ulrike Kammerhofer-Aggermann**, “Ikonologische Marginalien. Die Bilder der Handschrift 1 zur Untersbergsage,” in the same volume (1992), pp. 219–266, especially p. 222, treats the illustrated manuscript designated Handschrift 1 and may clarify codicology, image/text sequence, and the provenance of the inscription.[5]
3. **Johannes Lang**, “Das Erbe der ‘Lazarusgeschichte’. Zur Entstehung und Instrumentalisierung der Untersbergsage,” _Mitteilungen der Gesellschaft für Salzburger Landeskunde_ 150 (2010), pp. 125–178, supplies a later source-critical account and an appendix.[6] PDF: https://www.zobodat.at/pdf/MGSL_150_0125-0178.pdf .
4. **Maria E. Dorninger**, “Mythische Endzeitvorstellungen. Der Untersberg und mittelalterliche Weissagungsliteratur” (year unverified; online paper), pp. 3–4, places the legend among imperial and end-time prophecies and reports a 1623 print of the Lazarus narrative.[7] PDF: https://www.plus.ac.at/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/1157260.pdf .
5. **Alexander Schöppner**, _Sagenbuch der Bayerischen Lande_, vol. 1 (Munich, 1852), “Ein Wanderer in den Untersberg,” pp. 5–8, prints a shorter initials tradition, including a form of “S.O.R.C.E.J.S.A.T.O.M.”[8] Scan: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/5b/Sagenbuch_der_Bayerischen_Lande_1.pdf .
6. **Wilhelm Herzog**, _Die Untersbergsage nach den Handschriften untersucht und herausgegeben_ (1929), pp. 27–50, remains the baseline diplomatic collation; the six-line HS 2398 item and neighboring witnesses are on pp. 28–29.[9] Scan: https://archive.org/details/herzog_untersbergsage .

### Prior decipherment claims

A public-facing page prints both a six-line HS 2398 transcription and the separate initials sequence “S.V.R.C.E.T.S.A.T.V.S.” with the expansion “Surget Satum”; it also says the familiar expanded phrase is not present in the six-line text.[10] That makes **SURGET SATUM a lead for a sibling initials tradition, not an expansion of symA**. The page's author, date, and pagination are unverified: https://www.untersberg-news.at/mystischer-untersberg/die-felsinschrift-untersbergcode/ .

Klaus Schmeh likewise discusses a 1623 initials form, “S.O.R.C.E.I.S.A.T.O.M.”; the article is unpaginated and the cited print was not inspected during this pass, so both the exact transcription and its relationship to HS 2398 remain unverified.[11] Klaus Schmeh, “Der Untersberg-Code: Ein ungelöstes Rätsel aus den nördlichen Alpen” (2014), page unverified: https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2014/05/15/der-untersberg-code-ein-ungeloestes-raetsel-aus-den-noerdlichen-alpen/ .

An online claim that Yve Kupka resolved the “Untersbergcode” in 2015 did not expose a citable method, glyph table, or page-level source in accessible material. Author, date, page, method, and result are **unverified**: https://untersberg.org/untersbergcode.html .[12] It should not enter the reading unless a reproducible publication can be obtained.

## 3. Other manuscript witnesses

**Most concrete lead:** Lang reports that around 1990 a manuscript, apparently of the eighteenth century, was offered for sale to the City of Bad Reichenhall and directs readers to an appendix.[6] The precise page, present location, accession status, and whether it is outside Herzog's twelve are **unverified**. Check Lang's appendix and footnotes against Herzog's sigla, then ask the Stadtarchiv Bad Reichenhall for the offer file, photographs, seller description, and any accession or rejection record.

A practical witness search should proceed from Weber-Fleischer's 1992 census rather than from web lists:

- Extract every siglum, shelfmark, owner, and provenance in Weber-Fleischer pp. 17–170 and Lang pp. 125–178.
- Reconcile those entries against Herzog pp. 27–50, marking aliases and copies of copies.
- Query the Salzburg Museum, Salzburg Landesarchiv, and Stadtarchiv Bad Reichenhall under **Lazarus Gitschner/Günzner**, **Untersberg**, **Propheceyung**, and the opening words rather than under “code.”
- Treat web claims of eighteen surviving versions as **unverified** until a shelfmark-level census supports the number.

This route may locate a clearer witness, but it may also show that a later manuscript merely repeats the shorter initials tradition. That distinction should be recorded before any character-level comparison.

## 5. Triage for symA and line 6

### Highest-yield next move

Build a **same-scribe abbreviation concordance** across all 28 IIIF leaves before adding another language model:

1. Segment every superscript, flourish, suspension point, macron, and loop in Lazarus Gitschner's hand.
2. Cluster by ductus: entry stroke, loop direction, exit stroke, relation to the baseline, and whether a neighboring minim belongs to the sign.
3. For each cluster, record full-word contexts elsewhere on the leaves where an expansion is constrained by syntax or an unabbreviated duplicate.
4. Only then compare the cluster with Cappelli and Walther.

This directly tests whether symA is a stable graph, two touching graphs, a letter plus abbreviation mark, or damage/retouching. Generic shape lookup cannot make that distinction.

### Line 6 tests

- Keep **lo[symA]mi** and **vmult** as diplomatic observations. Treat “loqui vult” as a content-driven hypothesis, not a transcription.
- Test whether the apparent **m** in “vmult” is a separate minim group, a nasal mark displaced from the preceding token, or part of symA-rev. Compare the scribe's secure **mi**, **mu**, **ni**, **ui**, and **ul** sequences elsewhere.
- Acquire the highest-resolution IIIF region and inspect separate color channels and local contrast; if the museum can supply it, oblique-light or multispectral imaging could distinguish ink from abrasion. Availability is **unverified**.
- Run a blinded forced-choice exercise with several paleographers: crop-only classification, then line context, then full-page context. Preserve each stage so later semantic convergence is visible.
- Use the shorter initials witnesses only as transmission evidence. They are too structurally different to assign a value to a repeated internal ligature in HS 2398.

### Confidence and falsifiers

- **High confidence:** Cappelli plus Walther is a better reference path than a small list of common Latin signs; Weber-Fleischer 1992 and Lang 2010 are directly relevant scholarly leads; Schöppner demonstrates a printed shorter-initials tradition.[1][2][4][6][8]
- **Medium confidence:** the manuscript offered to Bad Reichenhall could add a witness absent from Herzog. This falls if Lang's appendix maps it to one of Herzog's twelve.[6]
- **Low confidence:** “SURGET SATUM” helps with HS 2398 symA; the accessible source itself separates that phrase from the six-line transcription.[10]
- **Very low confidence:** the 2015 solution claim is usable evidence. It becomes relevant only if a page-cited, reproducible glyph-by-glyph method is produced.[12]

A decisive result would be a same-hand occurrence of symA inside a word whose expansion is independently recoverable. Until that exists, dictionary resemblance, witness initials, and plausible Latin all remain leads rather than readings.
